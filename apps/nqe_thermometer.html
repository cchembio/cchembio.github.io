1D quantum thermometer Zero-point delocalization and the classical limit

Quantum spread / classical spread
—

How much more spread out the quantum particle is than a classical one at the same temperature. At high temperature the two agree. As the temperature falls, the classical particle settles into the bottom of the well while the quantum one keeps its zero-point spread, so the ratio grows. Heavier isotopes stay closer to classical.

Averages over the live simulations — they settle within a few seconds and restart whenever a control changes.

Temperature is given in units of ħωH/kB, the harmonic well's energy quantum for hydrogen. Below about 0.3 quantum effects dominate; above about 1 the particle is nearly classical. In the panels, energies are in the same units and positions in units of √(ħ/mHωH), the length scale of hydrogen's zero-point motion in the harmonic well.
Both particles are jostled by the same kind of random thermal kicks. The classical one is a single point; where it is found does not depend on its mass, only how fast it moves. The quantum one is a ring of 32 beads joined by springs — the same method used in real quantum simulations. Both results carry a little statistical noise.
