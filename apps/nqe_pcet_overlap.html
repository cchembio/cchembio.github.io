The double sum runs over the lowest four vibrational states on each side. Only the overlap Sμν — the shaded area above — depends on distance; pulling the donor and acceptor apart by a few tenths of an Ångström thins it toward zero and the rate falls by orders of magnitude. Deuterium, being more compact, loses its overlap faster: that is where the kinetic isotope effect comes from.

Gating stands for thermal motion in the donor–acceptor distance itself — a slow heavy-atom vibration that stretches and compresses the hydrogen bond. Rather than evaluate k at one fixed R, this averages it over a Gaussian spread of R centred on the slider value, with standard deviation σ set by Gating amplitude. Because k(R) falls off so steeply, that average is dominated by the shortest, fastest instants in the spread — not by the mean R — which is why gating raises the rate and, by partly erasing the very R-dependence that separates the isotopes, collapses the KIE.
